\begin{abstract}
We study the problem of \emph{live-updating} a known, high-fidelity 3D Gaussian Splatting (GS) scene from asynchronous event streams captured by a \emph{static} event camera with known pose. Unlike prior event-based novel-view synthesis works that reconstruct static or dynamic scenes from scratch under a moving-camera assumption, we exploit the event camera's microsecond temporal resolution to model \emph{dynamic increments} on top of a frozen baseline scene, decoupling fidelity (from the baseline) from temporal resolution (from events). This setting---a fixed sensor monitoring a dynamic 3D asset---maps directly onto digital-twin and AR/VR scene-live-update applications. We propose EventGS, a three-layer framework: (1) a feed-forward event-conditioned decoder that maps an event voxel grid to per-Gaussian polynomial motion increments; (2) an online analysis-by-synthesis refinement that optimizes increments against a differentiable event-consistency loss derived from the event-generation model $\Delta\log I = \pm C$, supervised bidirectionally on both event and non-event pixels; and (3) an \emph{observability-aware} intermittent keyframe alignment that resolves the aperture problem. The key technical contributions are a per-Gaussian observability score, computed from the structure tensor of the baseline render, that (i) weights the event loss away from the degenerate no-motion minimum along straight edges, and (ii) triggers keyframes adaptively---only when the event stream can no longer disambiguate the motion. We further introduce a Contrast-Maximization regularizer on the predicted 2D Gaussian flow that provides a keyframe-free, edge-normal aperture-breaking signal in every window. We instantiate the prototype on a differentiable orthographic Gaussian rasterizer and evaluate on three synthetic benchmarks isolating the aperture problem. The full model recovers correct-sign motion on a pure straight-bar translation where event-only recovery is at chance (sign-agreement $0.53 \to 0.99$ with keyframe, $0.53 \to 0.64$ with CMax alone), while the adaptive scheduler requests $\sim$$100\times$ fewer keyframes than fixed-cadence alignment.
\end{abstract}
